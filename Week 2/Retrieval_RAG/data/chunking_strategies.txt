Chunking Strategies in RAG

Chunking is the process of splitting a large document into smaller pieces before embedding and storing them. The chunk is the unit of retrieval — whatever you chunk becomes the smallest retrievable piece of information.

Fixed chunking splits text every N characters or tokens regardless of content. It is simple and predictable but often splits sentences or paragraphs mid-thought, which hurts both embedding quality and answer readability. Fixed chunking works acceptably for highly structured content like tables or code but is generally the worst choice for prose.

Recursive chunking is the standard approach for prose documents. It tries to split on natural boundaries in order: paragraphs first (double newline), then single newlines, then sentences, then words, and only then individual characters as a last resort. This preserves semantic units as much as possible. LangChain's RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter implements this pattern and is the default recommendation for most use cases.

Chunk overlap is a technique where the last M characters of one chunk are repeated at the start of the next chunk. This prevents information loss at boundaries — without overlap, a sentence that spans a chunk boundary would be split across two chunks, making neither retrievable for queries about that sentence. Typical overlap is 10-15% of chunk size.

Chunk size affects retrieval precision and context richness. Small chunks (128-256 tokens) are precise — they match narrow queries well — but may lack surrounding context, making the LLM's answer incomplete. Large chunks (1024+ tokens) provide rich context but match queries less precisely and consume more tokens per retrieval. The standard starting point is 512 tokens with 50-100 token overlap.

Semantic chunking is an advanced technique that splits on embedding similarity rather than character boundaries. It embeds every sentence, then splits where the cosine similarity between adjacent sentences drops below a threshold — indicating a topic change. This produces more coherent chunks but is slower to index and overkill for most production systems.

The right chunk size depends on your documents and queries. Short factual queries need small precise chunks. Long analytical queries need larger context-rich chunks. The only way to know is to evaluate retrieval quality empirically on real queries from your use case.
